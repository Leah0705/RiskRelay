The cutoff is the product

One trained model. One score per event. The only thing that moves below is where you cut the ranked list, and it takes the reviewers’ daily queue from roughly four thousand alerts to under a hundred. Nothing about the model changes.

Measured on the held-out chronological test split: 75,000 events over 4.55 days, 1,762 of them high-risk. Every point is a real evaluation of the trained XGBoost model, not a simulation. The preset points match reports/operating_points.json (regenerate with python scripts/operating_points.py). The slider steps in hundredths, so the presets are rounded: the pipeline’s own tuned values are 0.6629 and 0.8084.

What you are moving

The model scores every event once. The cutoff decides which of those scores becomes an alert, so the cutoff sets how much work lands on the team each day.

Drag it left and the queue floods: at 0.50 a team would face about 4,199 alerts a day and waste roughly 18 reviews for every real catch. Drag it right and the queue becomes staffable, at the cost of the high-risk events you never look at.

That is a staffing decision, not a modelling one, which is the whole argument the project was built to make.

Why the numbers are low

This runs on synthetic data, and the generator builds labels in two passes: it plants 850 positives that separate cleanly on the PCA features, then flips a further 10,906 normal rows to positive so the counterparty and device features correlate with something.

So 93% of the positives look exactly like normal traffic in the feature space. Scored separately, the model reaches 0.691 PR-AUC on the positives that carry signal and 0.048 on the rest. The headline 0.112 mixes the two.

The pipeline is not the weak part here. The label is.
